### Stage 3's distinct hashes

The same ledger of 100,000 occurrences, 50 shingle rows each, 4,500,000 rows of 3,602,000 distinct hashes, 2,000 of them seen in two or more survivors; and one of 20,000 occurrences, 500 rows each, 9,000,000 rows of 7,220,000 distinct hashes, 20,000 of them seen twice or more. Four ways of writing stage 3's two tables, each in one transaction as the step is, each checked equal to the others row for row. These are the figures of 2026-10-08, and T6 is the record's first form, which §3 no longer decides:

- **M**: the survivors a page at a time (C), their rows in occurrence order, one map entry a distinct hash counted by the last occurrence seen, the rows of two or more written at the end. The best that holds the map.
- **T1**: a tally kept in SQLite, in `similarity`'s own `shingle_document_frequency` and `shingle_corpus_size`: for each page of survivors one `INSERT … SELECT … GROUP BY` of that page's rows, upserted onto the run's rows, and every hash seen once deleted at the end.
- **T2**: the same tally, each page's rows counted in Java and upserted in a batch.
- **T6**: one grouping by SQLite of every shingle row of the run, keeping the hashes two or more occurrences carry, and one of each granularity's shingled occurrences; then the run's shingled occurrences read from `shingle` a page of 1,000 at a time, each page asked of the ledger, and what those that do not survive contributed taken off.

- **T6 took 2.0 to 2.5 times M's time**, held a page in the heap, and wrote only the rows that stay. Its first form read the shingled occurrences by a page statement that SQLite planned on `shingle_by_run_id` with `USE TEMP B-TREE FOR DISTINCT`, sorting all the run's rows for every page: 34 s and 15 s for the take-off. The upsert and the delete are each a lookup by key for every hash of the page.

### 2. Candidate C, a page at a time

**The shape.** The caller goes through `Verdicts.survivors(run)` and gathers its ids into pages of at most 1,000, the ledger's own page size, in the order they come. For each page it reads that page's rows of its own table in one statement:

- `ConfidenceDistribution`: `SELECT occurrence_id, mean_score FROM extraction_metric WHERE run_id = ? AND occurrence_id IN (?, …)`, the run being stage 2's, and adds each score to its grade as it is read.
- `SeedCorpusComparison`: `MeasuredForms` gains `eachOf(RunId, Collection<OccurrenceId>, Row)`, which hands each row of the named occurrences under the run to the callback. `pipeline` passes `extraction.ExtractionMetrics.eachMeasuredFormOf(RunId, Collection<OccurrenceId>, MeasuredFormRow)`, new, whose statement is the seven columns `eachMeasuredForm` reads with `AND occurrence_id IN (?, …)`. `embedding` names no `extraction` table (ADR-209 §3.2's arrangement, one method more). `each` stays, for the seeds' rows under the measurement run, which are the seed set and are read as before.

No statement names more than 1,000 occurrences, so none comes near SQLite's limit on a statement's variables. A page with no survivor makes no statement.

**Why a page at a time, and not one statement beside a cursor.** The candidate as the ticket words it reads the other table in occurrence order alongside the survivors and merges the two. Done literally, that is two statements open at once: the read of the other table, and the ledger's page statements, which go through the template. ADR-193 §2 forbids exactly that inside a counted read: outside a transaction it borrows a second connection, and under the test profile's pool of one it waits on a connection its own caller holds. So the merge is made the other way round: the survivors' page is read first and let go of as a statement, then the other table's rows for that page. Each statement finishes before the next begins, and the merge is the same merge.

**Each module keeps its statement.** The lookups are written by the module that owns the table, as every statement over that table is (ADR-209 §3). The survivors are asked of `ledger`. Nothing passes a connection, a table's name or a temp table across a module.

### 4. The comparison's quartiles are exact over at most four reads

The corpus side is read through `MeasuredForms.eachOf`, a page of the measurement run's survivors at a time (§2), as many times as its quartiles need and at most four. The seeds' side is the seed set, and is held and sorted as today.

**The key of a value.** `long b = Double.doubleToLongBits(v); long key = b ^ ((b >> 63) | Long.MIN_VALUE);`, compared as unsigned. It orders every double as `Double.compareTo` does, which is the order `Quartiles.of` sorts by: a value below zero has all its bits turned, so the more negative comes first; a value at or above zero has its sign bit set, so it comes after every one below; -0.0 comes immediately before 0.0; and two values are the same value exactly when their keys are equal. The record's first form took the raw bits, which put every value below zero above every one above it. Nothing in `schema.sql` keeps a count from being negative, so the key does not rely on it.

**The first read** gives, for each of the four signals (word count, page count, vowelless-word ratio, single-character-word ratio, each skipped where it is undefined, as `MetricRow` has it): how many values there are; the least and the greatest; the first 1,000 values; and, in 65,536 counters, how many values share each value of their key's leading 16 bits. It also counts the corpus documents and the language and provenance categories, which need nothing more.

**Then each signal is answered.**

- **With no value**, it has no quartiles, as today.
- **With 1,000 values or fewer**, from the values kept, by `Quartiles.of`.
- **With its least and greatest the same value by key**, every quartile is that value. By key, and never by `==`, here and wherever this section says two values are the same: -0.0 and 0.0 are equal to `==` and are two values to the sort.
- **Otherwise**, the ranks `Quartiles.of` takes are known from the count: the median's one or two, and the same of each half, six at most. Each rank is found from the counters: the 16 bits its value's key begins with, how many values lie below them, and how many share them. Those that share the bits found so far are the rank's *candidates*.

**Each later read** does one of two things for each rank not yet answered:

- **a rank with 1,000 candidates or fewer has them gathered**, sorted by `Double.compareTo` and picked, and is answered;
- **a rank with more is narrowed**: its candidates are counted by the next 16 bits of their keys, in 65,536 counters, and the least and the greatest of them are kept. After the read, if the least and the greatest are the same value, the rank is that value and is answered. Otherwise it has 16 more bits of its key and a new, smaller set of candidates.

**So a quartile that many values share is answered in two reads**: all its candidates after the first read are that one value, where the first 16 bits tell it from its neighbours, and the second read finds their least and greatest equal. Page counts are the case, most documents having one page: 1.0 shares its leading 16 bits with no other whole number. **And there is never a fifth read**: a rank narrowed on the second, third and fourth reads has all 64 bits of its key after the fourth, and a key is one value.

**What the fourth read answers with.** A rank still being narrowed at the fourth read has its candidates counted by the last 16 bits of their keys, as at any other. Where their least and greatest are the same value, the rank is that value, as above. **Where they differ, the rank is found among those counts as at every earlier read, which gives the last 16 bits of its key, and it is answered with the value that key is**, however many candidates share it: for a key whose first bit is set the double of the bits `key ^ Long.MIN_VALUE`, and otherwise of `~key`, which undoes the key above. It is never answered with the least of the candidates, which is the value at the rank only when no candidate lies below it. The case is more than 1,000 values at each of two or more doubles whose keys differ only in their last 16 bits. Whole numbers below 2³⁶ cannot be that close, so no word count or page count of a real run reaches it; the two ratios can.

Ranks of all four signals are narrowed or gathered in the same reads. A later read holds 65,536 counters and two values for each rank being narrowed and at most 1,000 values for each rank being gathered: with six ranks to a signal, at most 1,572,864 counters, 6,291,456 bytes, about 6.3 MB at 10⁶ bytes to the MB, and 24,000 values, whatever the survivors. The quartiles are `Quartiles.of`'s own, the mean of two middle values where it takes two, so the figures and the statements on the page are the same as today's (Measured).

**Each read is reported as a read of its own** (§9): the first as `CORPUS_METRICS`, every later one as `EmbeddingStatement.CORPUS_METRICS_AGAIN`, new, declared straight after it, each started with the span of stage 2's rows, told its rows after each page, and ended. The seeds' `SEED_METRICS` comes after the last of them, as now.

### 5. Candidate B at 5f, and one partition at a time

**`Verdicts.survivingAmong(RunId run, Collection<OccurrenceId> occurrences)`** answers a `Set<OccurrenceId>`: those of the given occurrences that carry no blocking verdict under `run` or any run upstream of it (ADR-156), and that belong to `run`'s walk. It asks in statements of at most 1,000 occurrences each, and asks nothing for an empty collection, not even the runs in scope. Its statement is the survivors' anti-join with `id IN (?, …)` in place of the keyset, planned by the integer primary key (§8). It is `ledger`'s, beside `survivors` and `survivorCount`. Stage 3 asks it too (§3).

**`ClusteringTasklet` holds one partition at a time.** For each partition, in the order `Clustering.partitions` gives, it reads the members (`Clustering.membersOf`, timed as now), keeps those `survivingAmong(scoring, members)` answers, in the order `membersOf` gave them, reads their keys and clusters them, and lets the partition go before it reads the next. It reads no survivor of the scoring run otherwise.

**So its counter of the keys it reads is one for each partition**, `Stage 5f (clustering, cache keys read, partition <P> of <M>)`, over that partition's surviving members, opened when the partition's members are known and before its first key is read. The counter over every partition's members summed needed every partition's members before the first was clustered, and those are the scoring run's survivors. It takes the shape 5f's counter of pairs of blocks already has. A partition left with no member opens no counter, as it opens none over blocks, and is counted done as now. **The members of a partition are now read just before that partition is clustered**, where they were all read first, so the timed lines `reading the members of partition <P> of <M>` fall each before its partition's work and its group sizes.

### 8. The two reads in id order sort nothing

**Each page of `Verdicts.survivors` and of `Occurrences.occurrencesOf` is planned as a search of `file_occurrence` by its integer primary key, `rowid>?`, and names no temp B-tree.** The rows and their order are unchanged. Written as today, SQLite prefers the index on `walk_id` and sorts the walk on every page; compared as a value, `+walk_id`, the walk no longer chooses the index, and the page goes forward from the last id read until it has 1,000. `survivingAmong` is written the same way, so each ask is a lookup by key.

**What it costs instead.** A page goes forward by id and tests the walk of every row it meets, so a read goes once through the rows of other walks that lie in its way, at both ends. **Its first page** starts below every id, as the keyset does today, and goes through the rows of every walk recorded before its own to reach its first row: 0.005 s for 100,000 earlier rows (Measured). **Its last page** goes on through the rows of every walk recorded after its own to learn it has no more: 0.031 s and 0.05 s for 1,000,000 later rows. Each is paid once a read, and a read is made by every stage that goes through its survivors, so a small walk recorded among large ones, the seed folder's among the corpus's, pays it each time. It grows with the occurrences of the other walks the database keeps, and nothing here bounds it; at the sizes measured it is hundredths of a second where the shipped form's every page was a tenth. `ledger` is in no stage's implementation version, so this moves no run id of its own.

### 12. SQLite's temporary files go to the working directory

**The process sets SQLite's directory for temporary files to the working directory once, as it starts, before any connection pool exists.** `pipeline` gains an environment listener, `TemporaryFilesInTheWorkingDirectory`, which `VesperaApplication.main` registers after `WorkingDirectoryPreparer` and `WorkingDirectoryLock`, so that the directory exists and is this invocation's when it runs. On `ApplicationEnvironmentPreparedEvent` it reads `vespera.working-dir`; where that is blank it does nothing, as `WorkingDirectoryPreparer` does; otherwise it opens a connection of its own to no database, `jdbc:sqlite::memory:`, runs `PRAGMA temp_store_directory = '<the working directory's absolute path, each apostrophe in it doubled>'`, and closes the connection. Nothing in `application.yaml` changes, and no connection of the pool runs the pragma.

**It is registered last of the four, after `ProfileShapeCheck` too.** The four declare no order, so each hears the event in the order `main` adds it. Nothing `ProfileShapeCheck` does needs the setting: it loads `profile.yaml` and opens no database. Coming after it, the listener is not reached by a start that is refused, for a working directory in use or for a profile that does not load, so such a start opens no connection to SQLite and changes nothing of the process's; and the three already registered keep the places they had. `StartUpListenersAreRegisteredInOrderTest` holds the four in that order.

**The path is absolute because `--db-dir` need not be.** `vespera.working-dir` is `${user.dir}/.vespera` unless `--db-dir` or the property names another, and either may name a path relative to the current directory. SQLite keeps the string it is given and reads it whenever it opens a temporary file, so a relative one would be read against whatever the current directory is at that moment. The listener gives it the absolute path of the directory the other listeners used; `TemporaryFilesInTheWorkingDirectoryTest` names a relative directory and reads an absolute one back.

**What SQLite's documentation says of this pragma**, read on sqlite.org on 2026-10-09, and it is why the setting is made once and there:

- *"Query or change the value of the sqlite3_temp_directory global variable"*: it is one variable for the process.
- *"In practice, temp_store_directory should be set immediately after the first database connection for a process is opened. If the temp_store_directory is changed for one database connection while other database connections are open in the same process, then the behavior is undefined and probably undesirable."*
- *"Changing the temp_store_directory setting is not threadsafe. Never change the temp_store_directory setting if another thread within the application is running any SQLite interface at the same time. Doing so results in undefined behavior. Changing the temp_store_directory setting writes to the sqlite3_temp_directory global variable and that global variable is not protected by a mutex."*
- *"The value directory-name should be enclosed in single quotes."* and *"An error is raised if directory-name is not found or is not writable."*
- *"This pragma is deprecated and exists for backwards compatibility only. … This pragma may be omitted from the build when SQLite is compiled using SQLITE_OMIT_DEPRECATED."*

The listener's connection is the first the process opens and the only one open when the pragma runs, and no other thread has touched SQLite: the environment is prepared before any bean exists. **The documentation and the source disagree about a mutex.** The documentation says outright that the variable *"is not protected by a mutex"* (the third quotation above). The source of SQLite's `pragma.c`, read on its main branch on 2026-10-09, takes one around the change, `SQLITE_MUTEX_STATIC_TEMPDIR`, entered before the directory is checked and left after the variable is written. For what ships, this record relies on the documentation and not on the source: the listener changes the setting when no other connection is open and no other thread has called SQLite.

**Measured** (2026-10-09, on Windows, sqlite-jdbc 3.53.2.1): the pragma run so on a connection to no database, for a directory named `R&D o'clock dir`, and that connection closed; a Hikari pool then built over a database file, with nothing about temporary files in its URL or its properties, reported that directory from `PRAGMA temp_store_directory` on its first connection; a sorted read too large for memory on that connection, while it was open, had a file named `etilqs_…` in that directory and none in the system's temporary folder. A directory that does not exist was refused: `not a writable directory`. The setting outlived the connection that made it, and `PRAGMA temp_store_directory = ''` on any connection put the default back. Every temporary file of §3's grouping was written in the directory set (Measured).

**Why not a setting of the datasource**, in its URL or among Hikari's data-source properties, which is what this record said before the second gate. Three things, each enough:

- **Every connection the pool opens would run the pragma again**, and the shipped pool retires a connection every 35 seconds (`max-lifetime: 35000`), so a new connection would change the process's variable while stage 3's grouping sorts on another: what the documentation says never to do. It was not measured whether that fails, and it does not need to be: nothing in this record does it.
- **It would reach every test context.** `application-test.yaml` is layered over `application.yaml` and overrides no data-source property, so every connection of every context under the `test` profile would run the pragma for its `vespera.working-dir`, `.vespera` under the current directory where a test names none. Measured, with the key put into `application.yaml` for one run of the suite and taken out again: 1,425 tests run, **391 errors in 76 classes** and 29 failures, the first of the errors `not a writable directory` on a working directory that does not exist, and others `unable to open database file` in classes that open plain connections of their own, the process having been left pointing at a folder an earlier test had deleted. In a checkout that has a `.vespera` the pragma would be accepted, and the suite would write SQLite's temporary files into a working directory of the operator's; that was reasoned and not run.
- **The value would be at the mercy of the path.** A URL separates its parameters at `&`, and the driver puts a value into the pragma as it stands, quotes and all. The listener quotes the path itself.

**No test context hears the listener.** A test context is built by Spring's test support and not by `VesperaApplication.main`, so the listeners `main` registers are in none; the tests that start the application whole start the packaged jar, in a process of its own. `main` is called in the suite's JVM by one test, `StartUpListenersAreRegisteredInOrderTest`, to read what it registers, and that run is abandoned before any event is published. So the listener sets nothing in any test context, and `NoPoolSetsSqlitesTemporaryDirectoryTest` holds that no configuration file carries the setting and that a test-profile connection reports no directory.

**How `main` is called without starting anything.** The test calls `VesperaApplication.main` inside `SpringApplication.withHook`. Read in Spring Boot 4.1.1's source, `SpringApplication.run` asks the hook for a run listener after it has made the object that times the start (`Startup.create()`), called `SpringApplication.shutdownHook.enableShutdownHookAddition()`, made its bootstrap context and set the `java.awt.headless` system property, and before it publishes anything; the hook reads `getListeners()` there and throws `SpringApplication.AbandonedRunException`, which `main` takes for neither of its refusals and throws again. Of `main`, that runs the building of the `SpringApplication`, the registration, and that much of `run`, the headless property being one every Spring Boot test sets too. The first two of the four were left out of this list until the gate of the build, and were read in the sources jar again on 2026-10-09 (`SpringApplication.java`, lines 305 to 308): `enableShutdownHookAddition()` sets a flag in a static field of Boot's, for the JVM, and adds no hook by itself, Boot adding its shutdown hook only when an application context is registered with it, which a run abandoned here never reaches. The test holds the rest: it gives `main` a `--db-dir` that does not exist and finds it still missing, so no working directory was created and none locked; a listener the hook adds hears no event at all; and SQLite reports no directory afterwards. The third gate suggested throwing from a run listener's `starting` instead. `run` adds the hook's run listener after Boot's own, which by then has published the event that a start has begun, and Boot's logging listener answers that event with `LoggingSystem.beforeInitialize`, which for Logback adds a filter named `SUPPRESS_ALL_FILTER` to the logging context unless a start has already initialized it, for a start that here never happens; that was read in the source and not run, and the earlier place was taken.

**The listener's own tests change the setting in the suite's JVM, which is what the documentation above forbids, and this is accepted for the suite on three conditions.** `TemporaryFilesInTheWorkingDirectoryTest` calls the listener four or five times and puts the default back before and after each test, while the Spring contexts cached across test classes hold connections open. Each condition was checked on 2026-10-09 against the tree:

- **The suite runs one test at a time, in one JVM.** `pom.xml` gives surefire an agent and an exclusion and no `parallel`, `forkCount` or `reuseForks`, and `src/test/resources` has no `junit-platform.properties`.
- **No thread of a cached context's pool calls SQLite while a test runs.** The test profile's pool keeps one connection and never retires it or drops it for being idle (`max-lifetime: 0`, `idle-timeout: 0`, both in `application-test.yaml`). **Nor does it check it, and that rests on a third setting, which is the shipped file's**: Hikari 7.0.2 checks an idle connection every two minutes, or up to a fifth sooner, unless `keepalive-time` says otherwise, on its housekeeping thread, with the shipped `connection-test-query`, and a lifetime of 0 does not turn that off, as read in Hikari's source. `application.yaml` sets `keepalive-time: 0`, for a reason of its own, and the test profile inherits it. `NoPoolSetsSqlitesTemporaryDirectoryTest` holds all three values on the pool a test context builds, so taking any of them out of either file fails there. The two context classes that are not under the test profile, `WriteAheadDatabaseTest` and `LockedDatabaseFileTest`, close their context when the class ends, and the pools the tests build by hand are closed by the tests that build them.
- **The connections left open are idle, and the default is back before the next test starts.**

**That is still short of the documentation**, which calls a change made while other connections are open undefined whatever those connections are doing. It is accepted for the suite's JVM and for nothing shipped. Should the suite come to run tests in parallel, or in more than one thread, or a pool of the test profile come to touch its connection from a thread of its own, the listener's test has to run in a JVM of its own. `StartUpListenersAreRegisteredInOrderTest` changes nothing and so needs none of this.

**When the working directory does not exist yet.** It always does by then: `WorkingDirectoryPreparer` creates it on the same event, and is registered first. Where it cannot be created that listener throws, as today, before this one is reached. If the pragma is refused all the same, the listener throws, as `WorkingDirectoryPreparer` does for a directory it cannot create, and `main` throws it on, so the start ends there; what it throws carries the working directory's absolute path in its message. A process that went on would write its temporary files to the system drive without saying so. `TemporaryFilesInTheWorkingDirectoryTest` calls the listener alone for a directory that does not exist, and holds that it throws, that the message has the path, and that no directory is set afterwards. **Which exception it is this record left to the build, and the build chose**: an `IllegalStateException` whose message is `could not set SQLite's directory for temporary files to the working directory at <the absolute path>`, with SQLite's own exception attached as its cause. No test holds the type or the cause. How a start that ends on it reads to the operator beyond that message, this record does not say and nothing was run to see.

**How the listener reaches SQLite's driver.** As built, it loads `org.sqlite.JDBC` by name, `Class.forName`, before it asks `DriverManager` for its connection, and a driver class that cannot be loaded ends the start with the same `IllegalStateException`. This record had left that to what the packaged jar showed: the listener runs before any pool has loaded the driver, under the launcher's class loader. **Whether `DriverManager` would find the driver there unaided is still not measured**: the listener was built with the load in it, and no start of the jar was made without it. So the load is a precaution whose need is unknown, and this record does not say the driver is not found without it.

**The pragma is deprecated.** The SQLite that sqlite-jdbc 3.53.2.1 bundles has it. A bundle built without it would have no such pragma, and SQLite says nothing of a pragma it does not know; the test of `TemporaryFilesInTheWorkingDirectoryTest` that reads it back after the listener has run is what would fail.

**What moves with it.** Stage 3's files, which are as large as the run (§3), and not only those: every temporary file SQLite writes for any statement of an invocation, the sorts of every timed read in ADR-193 §6 and stage 4b's build of `shingle_by_hash` among them. That build's temporary files measured about 91 bytes a row, 392.3 MB for 4,500,000 rows, so for the 42,833,917 rows stage 4b's line stated on 2026-10-04 they would be about 3.6 GB by that arithmetic, 3.9 billion bytes, **which the disk holding the working directory now has to have free, where the system drive had to before**. **The operator accepted that on 2026-10-09, with that figure**: every temporary file of the process in the working directory, stage 4b's among them. What was accepted is where the files land. Their size is as it was before this record, and whether that build meets ADR-060's bound is not settled here (What this does not decide). The database file, its write-ahead log and its shared-memory file are where they were.

## Alternatives refused

- **Candidate B for the reads of metrics.** Measured: no cheaper, and over shingles 11 to 56 times C's statements and the slowest of the three.
- **One statement over the other table beside the ledger's cursor.** Two statements open at once on one connection, which ADR-193 §2 forbids inside a counted read and the pool of one turns into a wait on itself (§2).
- **The survivors written into a temporary table and joined in the owning module's SQL.** That module's statement would then name a table of `ledger`'s, and a temporary one belongs to one connection, so it would hold only inside a transaction.
- **Counting the paged reads by SQLite's steps across their pages.** Each page is its own statement with its own handler, and a page of extraction metrics takes about 12,000 steps, under one callback, so the read would never report. The module reads every row in Java and knows the count exactly.
- **5f's counter kept summed, by reading every partition's members twice.** Twice the statements and twice each partition's timed lines, to keep a total over a collection this record exists to stop holding.
- **Stage 3's map kept, counted by the last occurrence seen (M).** The fastest measured, and it holds about 146 bytes for every distinct hash of the corpus: 1,053 MB of heap, at 10⁶ bytes to the MB, for 9,000,000 shingle rows. The operator's framing is a bound whatever the size, and this has none.
- **A tally of every hash kept in SQLite a page at a time (T1, T2).** Bounded in the heap, and measured at 5 to 16 times M's time for T1 and 28 to 37 times for T2, with a write-ahead log and a file growth the size of the corpus's distinct hashes, nearly all of them written to be deleted.
- **A table of `similarity`'s own for the tally.** Not needed: the two tables the counts end in have the keys the statements name, and §3 writes only rows that stay. A new table would bring DDL, a schema version and an existing working directory's upgrade for nothing measured.
- **The grouping in slices of the hash, one statement a slice**, each slice's files bounded: the operator's first answer, and this record's form for a day. Each slice reads every row of the run. Sized by the run's rows at 4,000,000 to a slice, which keeps a slice's files under 100 MB, they took 1.1 to 1.8 times one statement's time at 4.5, 9 and 27 million rows, and their number grows with the run; at a fixed 256 they took 161.5 s for 4.5M rows where one statement took 3.4 s. The operator set them aside for one statement (Rests on).
- **Slices by ranges of occurrence, or of rowid.** A hash's rows lie in every such range, so the ranges' counts would have to be added in a table: T1, refused above.
- **One statement that says nothing while it runs**, the record's first form, timed as ADR-193 §1 times a statement that sorts. The operator asked to see how far it has got, and the callbacks were measured to come (§9).
- **The temporary directory as a setting of the datasource**, in its URL or among its properties: this record's form before the second gate. Run again by every connection the pool opens, against SQLite's documentation; reaching every test context, 391 errors measured; and at the mercy of the characters in the path (§12).
- **One statement reported in two parts, the rows read and then the rows counted.** The reading took 8 steps a row over the 4.5 million rows, so its share might be stated exactly, and on a slow disk the reading is where the time goes. But nothing a test can see tells the first loop's steps from the second's: the 8 was found by watching the temporary files stop growing, and over the 9 million rows they gave no clean mark at all. A figure no test holds could go wrong with a newer SQLite and put the share ahead of the work, so the one ceiling over the whole statement, which a test does hold, was taken.
- **The steps themselves on the line, or the seconds.** Neither says how far (§9).
- **The check's pages read from `shingle`**, the record's first form. Measured: every page goes through every stage-2 run's rows, 1.2 s more for three more runs over 4.5M rows, and the table gains a run each time stage 2's id moves.
- **The corpus size by a grouping of the run, with the ruled-out taken off.** One more read of every row of the run, a sort the size of its shingled occurrences, and a row of zero for a granularity no survivor carries (§3).
- **The take-off as an `UPDATE … FROM`.** Measured at half the upsert's time on these ledgers, and planned as a search of the run's whole frequency table for every page: a cost that grows with the hashes seen twice or more, once a page, which the upsert's keyed form does not have.
- **SQLite's temporary storage kept in memory**, `temp_store = MEMORY`. It moves every sort into the process's memory outside the Java heap, the size of a run for stage 4b's build: the opposite of a bound.
- **Estimated quartiles**, from a sketch or a histogram. Bounded and one read, and the figures on the page would change; the operator chose exact (Rests on).
- **The quartiles found by SQLite, by `ORDER BY … LIMIT 1 OFFSET`.** The population is the survivors, and `extraction`'s SQL cannot name them (ADR-209 §3).
- **The quartiles' key kept as the raw bits, and the counts required never to be negative.** Nothing in the schema requires it, and the key that needs no such rule costs one shift and two bit operations a value.

## Consequences

- **None of the six holds a set of a run's survivors**, nor anything else in the heap that grows with the corpus, but the seed set and 5f's largest partition (§10).
- **Stage 3 writes temporary files as large as the run is long, with no bound, in the working directory**: about 22 to 23 bytes a shingle row, 98.9 MB for 4.5 million rows, 190.9 MB for 9 million and 587.5 MB for 27 million (§3). They are the one exception to ADR-060's bound, made by the operator's choice for this statement alone.
- **Every temporary file SQLite writes moves to the working directory** (§12), stage 4b's build's among them, about 91 bytes a shingle row of the table, so the disk that holds the working directory needs the room the system drive needed.
- **Start-up does one thing more**, and fails if SQLite will not take the working directory for its temporary files (§12).
- **Every read of the survivors and of a walk's occurrences stops going through the walk on every page** (§8): the read of a run's survivors and their metrics measured at about a fifteenth of the time at 100,000 occurrences, 1.29–1.35 s to 0.08–0.09 s, and about a fiftieth at 300,000, 12.98 s to 0.24 s, and the gap grows with the walk. Every stage that reads its survivors gains it, not only the six. A read goes once through the rows of the other walks at each end (§8).
- **Stage 3's document frequency takes 2.0 to 2.7 times what the map would at the sizes measured** (Measured, §3): 5.7 to 6.4 s against 2.5 to 2.9 s for 4.5M rows, and 12.6 to 13.5 s against 4.9 to 5.3 s for 9M, on a solid-state disk. **How long it takes on `H:` is not known.** Reading a run's rows took half an hour there (ADR-191); the grouping reads them once, as that read did, then sorts them and reads them back from its temporary files, and the check reads the walk's occurrences' rows once more by key.
- **Stage 3 says how far its grouping has got, as a share that runs behind and ends short of a hundred** (§9), where the record's first form said nothing while it ran; and it counts the walk's occurrences as it checks them.
- **5b reads the corpus side up to four times**: three in each probe of 240,000 survivors or more, two where every quartile not found at once is tied or has 1,000 candidates or fewer, each a read like the one it makes today.
- **5c and 5d go through their survivors in occurrence order.**
- **The operator sees four pairs of timed lines fewer; two pairs say *counting* where they said *reading*; stage 3's first line is reworded and its progress lines take a new name and a new count; the two reads of extraction metrics made a page at a time write a progress line on small corpora too; 5b's later reads write lines of their own; stage 3's frequency-rows counter goes and a running counter comes; and 5f's keys counter is one for each partition** (§9).
- **The run ids of stages 2 to 6b move once**, with the caches warm (§11).
