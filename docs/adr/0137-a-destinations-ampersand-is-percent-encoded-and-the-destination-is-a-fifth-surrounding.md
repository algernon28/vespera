# ADR-137 — A destination's ampersand is percent-encoded, and the destination is a fifth surrounding

- **Date**: 2026-09-21
- **Status**: accepted
- **Amends**: [ADR-135](0135-a-membership-entry-links-relatively-or-does-not-link-at-all-and-the-citation-anchor-stands.md) — one sentence of its Decision §5 (*"A bare `&` needs nothing"*), and the conclusion that sentence carried, that the destination is not one of the positions ADR-134 enumerates. Everything else in that record stands: an entry still links by a path relative to its own page or carries no link at all, the two directories still decide which case applies, a document's own name still never goes through `Path`, and the citation anchor still stands. Also amends [ADR-136](0136-the-angle-bracket-and-the-ampersand-are-backslash-escaped-and-the-heading-becomes-a-fourth-surrounding.md) in one sentence of its Consequences — *"There are now four surroundings, not three"* — which this record makes five. **The two false sentences ADR-136 carries about the heading's brackets and about `onOneLine`'s callers are deliberately not touched here**: they belong to [#258](https://github.com/algernon28/vespera/issues/258) and travel with its own record.
- **Rests on**: [`docs/research/deliverable-markdown-rendering.md`](../research/deliverable-markdown-rendering.md) **§8**, measured on 2026-09-21 across `markdown-it` 15.0.2 (both `html` settings), `marked` 18.0.13, the `commonmark` 0.31.2 reference (both `safe` settings) and GitHub's live `POST /markdown` in both modes — seven configurations, every fixture generated by running the shipped writer and read back from the file it wrote. Also on §6b, the encoding the destination already applies, and §7, what the same character gets in the link text beside it. And on [ADR-109](0109-a-citation-is-an-exemplar-ordinal-minted-for-one-call-and-the-check-is-that-it-is-in-range.md) — the chain a membership entry is the second click of — and [ADR-134](0134-a-line-break-in-a-value-is-folded-and-three-escaping-rules-stand-because-there-are-three-surroundings.md), whose rule about surroundings this record applies rather than contradicts.
- **Settles** [#259](https://github.com/algernon28/vespera/issues/259).

## Context

ADR-135 settled that a membership entry links to the original by a path relative to the page carrying it, and ADR-109 states the chain that serves: a citation is one click from its membership entry, and the membership entry is one click from the original. **Where a document's name contains an entity-shaped run, that second click lands on a file the archive does not hold.**

Measured, and generated by running the shipped writer over an `OccurrencePath` of `reports/&copy; notes.pdf` (§8a), the tool emits:

```
1. <a id="document-1"></a>[reports/\&copy; notes.pdf](../../../../archive-that-is-not-there/reports/&copy;%20notes.pdf)
```

`java.net.URI` does not quote `&` — it is legal in a path segment — and CommonMark recognises entity and numeric character references in link destinations as much as anywhere else outside code. So the destination is decoded before it is resolved, and **seven configurations of seven route the reader to `reports/© notes.pdf`** (§8b): six decode it into the character, and `marked` passes `&copy;` into the `href` untouched, where the browser's own attribute parser decodes it instead. Nothing reports an error. The entry names the right document and leads somewhere else, which is the failure mode hardest for a reviewer to notice.

### Why the text was swept and the destination was not

ADR-136 closed this exact shape in the position a value is **read**: `\&copy;` displays as `&copy;`, the characters the archive really holds, in all seven. Its sweep was over the positions that record counted — a cell, a link's text, a heading, the CSV — and the destination was not among them, because ADR-135 §5 had said outright that the destination adds no escaping rule and that a bare `&` in one needs nothing. That sentence measured the wrong thing: it established that a raw `&` arrives in the `href` as an `&`, and stopped there, which is true of an `&` that begins nothing and false of one that begins an entity name.

So the destination was the one position a value lands in that no record had enumerated, and it is the one position the sweep missed. That is not a coincidence, and §4 below is the part of this decision that is about it rather than about the character.

## Decision

### 1. An ampersand in a destination is percent-encoded as `%26`, and it joins `(` and `)` rather than starting anything new

`relativeDestination` already escapes two characters the URI grammar permits in a path, on a ground the URI grammar knows nothing about: **a Markdown destination ends at the first unescaped `)`**, so `(` and `)` become `%28` and `%29` on top of the URI's own quoting. `&` is the same shape. The URI grammar permits it; the Markdown destination it is written into decodes an entity reference inside it; so what requires the escape is the destination's own grammar rather than the URI's.

This is therefore the existing precedent applied a third time, and this record is mostly here to say why that precedent reaches it. Measured (§8c), with `%26` written the destination arrives at the reader byte for byte in all seven configurations, `reports/%26copy;%20notes.pdf` among them, and there is nothing left in it for a renderer to decode.

What this does **not** cover, because nothing needs it to: the numeric form. `reports/&#169; notes.pdf` is already safe, and not by anybody's intent — `java.net.URI` quotes `#` to `%23`, so the tool emits `&%23169;`, which is no longer a character reference at all (§8b). The named form was the only live one, and it is the one this closes.

### 2. One replacement, and not a stricter encoder

A stricter encoder was considered, on the ground that it would cover a class rather than a character. It is refused on the measurement.

The class was enumerated rather than guessed at (§8b). NTFS permits every printable ASCII character except `< > : " / \ | ? *`; of the punctuation it permits, `java.net.URI`'s path quoting leaves fifteen unencoded — the thirteen `! $ & ' + , - . ; = @ _ ~` plus `(` and `)`, which `relativeDestination`'s own replacements then encode as §1 describes — so exactly thirteen punctuation characters reach the page unencoded, alongside every letter and digit, and every other character the sweep carried came out percent-encoded before it reached the page. Each of the thirteen was driven through all seven configurations as an emitted entry. **Twelve of them arrive in the `href` naming the same file the source named; `&` is the only one that does not.** A stricter encoder would encode twelve characters against no measured hazard, and would have to be re-measured against the filesystem resolve for each of them, to buy the one case a single replacement already buys.

It would also not be the JDK's off-the-shelf one. `URLEncoder` implements `application/x-www-form-urlencoded`, which is a different grammar: `URLEncoder.encode("reports/&copy; notes.pdf", UTF_8)` returns `reports%2F%26copy%3B+notes.pdf` (§8d), where the separator is gone and the space has become a `+`. A destination composed that way does not open the file, which is the one thing a destination has to do.

So the rule stays where it is and gains one member: `&` becomes `%26` alongside the two replacements already applied to what `toASCIIString` returns.

### 3. The ordering is free here, and that is worth stating because ADR-136's was not

ADR-134 and ADR-136 both turn on an order: a rule that inserts its own escape character escapes that character first, or the escape it adds merges with a literal one already present. Nothing of the kind applies here. `URI` has already quoted every `%` in the name to `%25`, so no `%` in the string begins an escape this rule could collide with; `%26` contains no parenthesis and `%28` and `%29` contain no ampersand; and so none of the three replacements can produce input for another. The three may run in any order.

This is recorded rather than left silent because the two records before it both turn on an ordering, and a reader arriving from them will look for one here.

### 4. The destination is a fifth surrounding, and its rule escapes by percent-encoding because it answers to a resolver

ADR-134 counted three surroundings and ADR-136 made them four: each one a position a value this tool did not compose is written into, each with a grammar of its own, each with a rule that guards exactly that grammar. **A link destination is such a position, it has such a rule, and it has had one since before either record was written** — the URI quoting plus `%28` and `%29`, and now plus `%26`. It was not counted, and not counting it is the mechanism of this defect: ADR-136 swept every surrounding it knew of, and this was the one it did not know of.

ADR-135 §5 is where that went wrong, and the amendment is narrow. It was right that no *new code* was needed then, and right that the text beside the destination keeps `escapeLinkText`'s rule. It was wrong to conclude from that that the destination is not one of the positions, which is what left it outside the next record's sweep.

Two objections to counting it were considered, and the second answers both:

- *Its escape is not a backslash.* Neither is the CSV's. `quoted` doubles a quote under RFC 4180 and has been one of the four from the start, so the count has never been a count of backslash rules.
- *It answers to a resolver rather than to a reader.* So does the CSV, which answers to a parser, and ADR-136 §5 leaves it alone for exactly that reason rather than striking it from the list. **What a surrounding answers to decides the form its rule takes, never whether it is one.** The three Markdown text positions answer to a reader and escape with a backslash; the CSV answers to a parser and escapes by doubling; the destination answers to a resolver and escapes by percent-encoding.

So there are five surroundings, and ADR-136's character class lands in every one of them, in the form that surrounding's rule takes: `\<` and `\&` in the cell, the list and the heading; `%3C` and `%26` in the destination, the first of them already written because the URI grammar forbids an angle bracket outright; and neither in the CSV.

The practical value of the count is a checklist. The next character found hazardous in a Markdown value has five positions to be asked about rather than four, and the answer for the fifth will not be a backslash.

### 5. The link text keeps its backslash, and nothing else moves

`escapeLinkText` is untouched. One entry now carries the same character twice, escaped two different ways, and that is the decision rather than an accident of it: the text is what a reader reads, so it says `\&copy;` and displays `&copy;`; the destination is what a resolver resolves, so it says `%26copy;` and opens `&copy; notes.pdf`. A rule applied to the whole entry instead of to its two halves would put a backslash into the route or a percent escape in front of the reader, and both are wrong.

Nothing in the ledger, the manifest or the index changes, and no schema moves. The index's own links are slugged to letters, digits and hyphens, and a citation's destination is `#document-n`, so a membership entry's destination remains the only one this tool composes out of a name it did not choose.

## Consequences

**A plain-text reader sees `%26` where the archive has `&`.** ADR-103 makes reading the raw `.md` a supported way to read the tree, so the cost is real, and it is the same trade ADR-136 took for the backslash rather than a different one. Two things bound it. It is not a new *kind* of cost in this field: the destination beside it already carries `%20` for every space, and `%23`, `%25`, `%5B` and `%28` for the rest, so a reader looking at a destination at all is already reading percent-escapes, and this adds one member to a list already in front of them. And that reader does not need the destination to learn the document's name — the entry's *text*, one line to the left of it, states the name in full and keeps its `&` legible under ADR-136. The two decisions divide the labour rather than contradicting each other, which is the answer to the reader who notices that one of them refused to encode this character and the other insists on it.

**The filesystem resolve is unchanged.** `%26` percent-decodes to `&` like any other escape, so the destination still opens the file: `URI.create("../corpus/reports/%26copy;%20notes.pdf").getPath()` returns `../corpus/reports/&copy; notes.pdf` (§8d). No configuration measured rewrites a destination, so what the reader follows is what the tool wrote.

**The count in two titles is now behind the record.** ADR-134's says three and ADR-136's says four; both are the reasoning that produced the next one rather than anything contradicted by it, and neither file is edited — `docs/adr/README.md` reopens a record only by a later record, and this is it.

**What stays open is what every record in this family has carried**: which renderers an operator actually opens the tree with. VS Code's preview, IntelliJ's, Obsidian and github.com in a browser are unmeasured as applications, and everything above rests on four libraries and one HTTP API. The decision does not have to choose between them — it is the form that survives all seven configurations measured, where the form it replaces survives none of them.
